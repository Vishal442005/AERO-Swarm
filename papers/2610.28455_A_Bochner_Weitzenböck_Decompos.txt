Abstract
We extend the Bochner–Weitzenböck decomposition to cellular sheaf Laplacians, splitting
it into a positive semidefinite term, the Bochner sheaf Laplacian, and a block diagonal term,
the Forman curvature. We show that the Bochner Laplacians form a class of cellular sheaves
that extends both connection and matrix-weighted Laplacians, and generalizes signed graphs to
d dimensions. We give a holonomy characterization of their kernel, prove the related Cheeger
inequalities, and attach to them a random walk that generalizes the Gremban expansion. For
the Forman term, we prove a discrete Bochner theorem, which bounds the first cohomology of a
cellular sheaf with nonnegative curvature, and we read the whole decomposition as an opiniondynamics model. We then apply this machinery to Sheaf Neural Networks, whose learnt Laplacian
is otherwise unobserved. The Bochner interpretation yields a node-level conviction score that acts
as an operational importance measure under frozen-model interventions, while the Forman profile
provides a local descriptor of the learnt operator.

Introduction

A sheaf prescribes how local data glue into global data, and turns the obstruction to gluing into a
cohomological invariant [1–4]. On a graph or a simplicial complex, a cellular sheaf [5, 6] attaches
a vector space to each cell and a linear map to each incidence, so that the theory becomes finite
linear algebra. In this form sheaves have been used for fusing sensor readings [7], for distributed
coordination and optimization [8, 9], and in topological data analysis [10–12], wherever a relation
on a graph needs more than a scalar weight. Their central operator is the sheaf Laplacian, which
has the foundations of a spectral theory [13] and a signal processing theory [14], and which models
opinion dynamics, the stalks carrying private opinions and the restriction maps the discourse between
agents [15].
In machine learning, where topology has entered from geometric deep learning [16] to higher-order
domains such as simplicial complexes and hypergraphs [17, 18], the most notable application of
sheaves is the Sheaf Neural Network [19, 20]. A graph neural network diffuses node features along
the edges, and fails on heterophilic graphs, where neighbours tend to carry different labels. A Sheaf
Neural Network learns a sheaf over the graph and diffuses with its sheaf Laplacian, so that each node
transforms its message differently for each neighbour, which restores separation power on heterophilic
data. The sheaf is then an object produced by training. The limiting behaviour of sheaf diffusion is
described by the Hodge decomposition, which identifies the kernel of the sheaf Laplacian with sheaf
cohomology. Three questions that are standard for the graph Laplacian, however, remain open for
a general sheaf Laplacian. The first is spectral : a general spectral theory of cellular sheaves is still
far from complete [13, 14], and Cheeger-type bounds, classical for graphs [21], are available only in
special cases, notably connection Laplacians [22, 23]. The second is probabilistic: it is not clear how

We address the three at once with a Bochner decomposition for cellular sheaves. Forman splits the
Hodge Laplacian of a complex into a positive semidefinite term and a diagonal curvature term,√using
the absolute value of the off-diagonal entries; replacing it by the matrix absolute value |A| := AA⊤
extends the construction to every symmetric block matrix, hence to every sheaf Laplacian, on a
graph or on a complex. We call Bochner matrices the class of Laplacians the first term belongs to. It
extends the connection Laplacians, the matrix-weighted Laplacians [25, 26] and the weighted signed
graphs [27]: a Bochner matrix is an anisotropic connection Laplacian, in which a positive semidefinite
matrix replaces the scalar weight of an edge. We show that its kernel admits the same holonomy
characterization known for connection Laplacians.
We then prove Cheeger inequalities for Bochner matrices, extending those of Bandeira, Singer and
Spielman for connection Laplacians [22]: they relate the low eigenvalues of the normalized Bochner
Laplacian to three frustration constants, the residuals of synchronization over Sd−1 , partial or full,
and over O(d). We also attach to a Bochner matrix a random walk on V × Sd−1 , whose jump law
depends on the orientation the walker carries. In the language of signed graphs it is a generalized
Gremban expansion, each node being lifted to a whole sphere instead of two copies, and in the isotropic
case it recovers the vector-valued walk of the connection Laplacian [23]. These two results answer the
spectral and the probabilistic question.
The Forman term answers the combinatorial one. It is block diagonal, each block computed from the
restriction maps at a single node. On 1-cells we call it the sheaf Forman Ricci curvature, and we prove
a Bochner theorem for cellular sheaves: under nonnegative curvature, the first sheaf cohomology of a
compact connected combinatorial n-manifold with d-dimensional stalks has dimension at most nd for
n ≤ 3, and at most d · D(M ) in any dimension, with D(M ) the local homological dimension of the
complex. Together, the two terms give the decomposition a dynamical reading that generalizes [28]:
sheaf diffusion can be seen as a reaction-diffusion process, the Bochner term diffusive and the Forman
term reactive. On a graph this is an opinion dynamics model, in which the Bochner term drives the
agents towards a common conviction, the norm a stalk carries, and the Forman block measures how
loudly an agent speaks relative to its neighbours, its relative volume.
We then return to Sheaf Neural Networks. The available theory describes what they can represent:
convergence of sheaf diffusion, and a universal approximation theorem by which a network can learn
any sheaf on a graph. What is missing is a description of what the learning mechanism does: which
region of the sheaf space a training pipeline reaches, and whether different pipelines are biased towards
different regions. From the decomposition we derive two diagnostics of neural sheaf diffusion, one
per term: the conviction and the Forman profile. The conviction is an operational score of node
importance: on a frozen trained network, removing the highest-conviction nodes is the most damaging
intervention at every budget and in every perturbation mode, while removing the lowest-conviction
ones costs less than removing arbitrary nodes of the same graph. The Forman profile tracks, layer
by layer, how a learning mechanism navigates the sheaf space, and each of the three mechanisms
we test leaves its own signature: normalized general sheaf learning converges to local alignment
with the normalized graph Laplacian, unnormalized learning aligns with neither reference, and joint
diffusion [29] reverses the role of normalization. Evaluated without normalization, the profile of the
same learnt maps stays far from that of the identity sheaf, so we read the alignment as an inductive
bias towards one region of the sheaf space.

1.1

Related work

Sheaves on directed and undirected graphs have recently been given a differential calculus [30], formalized for the Grothendieck topology and developed in the spirit of noncommutative geometry,
where a Bochner Laplacian is defined as ∇∗ ∇ for ∇ the connection of a bundle over a graph. We
work instead with cellular sheaves, hence over the Alexandrov topology, and our starting point is
the decomposition of an existing sheaf Laplacian. A recent work also brings Forman curvature and
sheaves together [31], building restriction maps as a function of the Forman curvature of the edges
and using the resulting sheaf Laplacian for community detection; the direction there is the opposite
of ours: we define a curvature from a cellular sheaf, while they use a graph curvature to define a
2

particular sheaf. Finally, dynamical processes on connection Laplacians have received considerable
attention lately [32–35], with a focus on Kuramoto models, Turing patterns and network coherence,
and our framework opens the possibility of carrying those results to the anisotropic setting while
still controlling the balance of the system through holonomy. We note that these works use the term
“matrix-weighted graph” to mean a connection Laplacian, whereas we reserve it for the sense of [25].
Curvature has also been studied on connection Laplacians independently of sheaves. Liu, Münch and
Peyerimhoff [36] define a Bakry–Émery curvature for connection graphs and derive higher-order Buser
inequalities from it, with Cheeger constants mixing the expansion of the underlying graph with the
frustration index of the signature. This notion of curvature was moreover reformulated in [37]. Such
curvatures are available on a connection Laplacian because it generates a random walk, with scalar
transition rates. For a general sheaf Laplacian it is not clear how to obtain one, and that failure is
exactly the Forman term, which measures how far the diagonal is from the sum of the magnitudes of
the off-diagonal blocks. Sheaf Laplacians with vanishing Forman term are the Bochner matrices, and
these, as we show in this work, do carry a random walk, so we believe a Bakry–Émery curvature can
be built for them, and we leave this to future work.
Sheaf Neural Networks were introduced by Hansen and Gebhart [19] on a sheaf fixed in advance;
Bodnar et al. [20] learn the sheaf from the data, and prove that a network can learn any sheaf
on a graph. Several works have since studied the parametrization of the restriction maps, through
connection Laplacians aligned with the data manifold [38], equivariant transport [39], or a diffusion
process on the restriction maps themselves, run jointly with the one on the node features [29]. Others
have extended the architecture, to features on SPD manifolds [40] and to message diffusion over
flat vector bundles [41]. The sheaf formalism has also been used to generalize attention in graph
neural networks [42]. On the theoretical side, oversmoothing has been related to a degeneration of
the representation underlying the learnt sheaf [43]. On the other hand, Hernandez Caralt et al. [44]
showed empirically that identity restriction maps perform similarly to learnt ones, which questions
to what extent learning the sheaf matters. Finally, crucial features of graph neural networks such
as over-squashing have been related to the curvature of the graph [45]. While we build a notion
of Forman curvature for cellular sheaves, we do not pursue this direction, which would involve the
Forman term of the sheaf Hodge Laplacian in degree 1; in Section 6.4 we compute instead the Forman
term of the graph sheaf Laplacian, in degree 0, which to avoid ambiguity we call Forman term and
not curvature. Studying over-squashing in Sheaf Neural Networks from this curvature perspective is
left for future work. We refer to Sections 6.2 and 6.4 for a further discussion.

Organization of the paper

The paper is organized as follows. Section 2 fixes notation and recalls cellular sheaves, signed graphs
and Forman’s decomposition. Section 3 introduces the Bochner decomposition for sheaf Laplacians,
and Sections 4 and 5 study its two terms in turn: within the first, Section 4.2 proves the Cheeger
inequalities and Section 4.3 attaches the random walk. Section 6 develops the dynamical reading
and the applications to Sheaf Neural Networks. We conclude in Section 7. Proofs deferred from the
body are collected in Appendix A, each statement being restated before its proof, further properties
of Bochner matrices are in Appendix B, and the experimental protocols with the additional material
are in Appendices C and D.

Definition 2.2 (Balance and antibalance). Γ is balanced if every cycle is positive, and antibalanced
if the signed graph obtained by reversing every edge sign is balanced.
Theorem 2.1 (Structure theorem for balance [49, 50]). Γ is balanced if and only if there is a partition
V = V1 ⊔ V2 such that every edge within V1 or within V2 is positive and every edge between V1 and
V2 is negative. It is antibalanced if and only if the same holds with the two signs exchanged.
