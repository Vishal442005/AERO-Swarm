Coordinating many miniature robots in a confined arena, with no leader, central planner, or
global map, is a core challenge for swarm robotics. As the swarm grows denser, collision
avoidance becomes the limiting factor, and the number of safely coexisting robots depends on
the formulation of the inter-robot safety constraint. We present a distributed predictive flocking
controller in which each robot solves its own nonlinear model predictive control (NMPC)
problem, optimizing swarm cohesion and alignment costs, inter-robot and obstacle safety, and a
task objective, while exchanging only predicted trajectories with local neighbors. Within this
generalized controller architecture, solved in real time, we compare six barrier and controlbarrier-function (CBF) safety formulations on a digital twin of an open-source vibration-driven
bristlebot in a 0.64 m2 arena. A look-ahead discrete-time CBF performs best overall, with the
fewest collisions and the widest inter-robot margins across nearly every swarm size tested.
We deploy it on phototaxis, phototactic orbiting, and cohesion without a stimulus tasks, using
swarm sizes starting from ten robots and up to the geometric packing limit. The swarm holds
well: collisions stay low in small swarms, rising steeply as the packing limit approaches, while
coordination and cohesion degrade gently rather than collapsing. The swarm is found not to
trade safety for order, keeping both across the confined arena, with local alignment high at every
swarm size.

1. Introduction
Autonomous multi-robot systems are a promising approach for tasks in cluttered, inaccessible, or hazardous spaces,
from confined-structure inspection to search in collapsed buildings and medical micro-robotics [1]. In these settings,
many miniature robots must coordinate in proximity within a tight, bounded arena. As in natural swarms, such as
flocking of starlings, schooling of fish, the crowded colonies of ants and honey bees, and even the collective migration
of cells, robust group behavior emerges from purely local interactions among simple agents [2, 3, 4], with no central
control [1]. Miniaturization, however, tightly bounds onboard compute, memory, and power [5], demanding control
that is theoretically principled yet computationally light. Moreover, when the arena is fixed, a growing number of robots
increases the density of the swarm and hinders smooth motion. When it is also cluttered with obstacles, coordination
is even harder. In this scenario, collision avoidance becomes the central challenge.
The standard way to coordinate such a swarm from purely local interactions is Reynolds’ boid model [6, 7], which
reduces collective motion to three local rules (cohesion, alignment, and separation) that produce coherent group motion
without global information [8]. Early control laws formalized these as artificial potential fields over a communication
graph [9, 10], and reactive velocity-matching rules scale to tens of drones [11]. Such reactive methods, however, offer no
predictive horizon, no formal safety guarantee under nonlinear dynamics, and no systematic handling of kinodynamic
limits. These shortcomings are especially severe in confined, high-density settings, where many underactuated robots
have little room to recover from unsafe motions.

Model predictive control (MPC) [12] addresses these limitations. For the nonlinear dynamics of mobile robots,
this takes the form of nonlinear MPC (NMPC). It solves a finite-horizon optimal control problem (OCP) online,
which provides closed-loop feedback, constraint satisfaction, and explicit multi-objective trade-offs. Distributed MPC
(DMPC) extends this to a swarm by splitting the global problem into per-agent OCPs coupled through shared
constraints [13, 14, 15, 16]. Solvers such as the Sequential Quadratic Programming real-time iteration (SQP-RTI) [17]
solve each per-agent OCP in real time. Similarly, optimization-based swarm planners achieve strong real-world
navigation [18, 19, 20, 21]. These methods are mainly developed for point-to-point navigation [14, 22], with collision
avoidance typically encoded as geometric constraints such as separating hyperplanes or safe corridors.
The closest works embed Reynolds behaviors in NMPC. They have been demonstrated on up to 16 quadrotors [23,
24], leader-follower teams [25, 26, 27], and fixed-wing unmanned aerial vehicles (UAVs) [28]. Most share one regime.
They assume sparse flight in large 3D airspace, with the swarm migrating toward a shared goal. Even when several
obstacles are present, they remain sparse relative to the available space, keeping the environment effectively lowdensity. Few address the opposite regime that we target, where many underactuated robots coordinate at high density
in a fixed, confined, and cluttered arena under a task objective such as following a light gradient. Here, the inter-robot
safety formulation, not free-space planning, governs how many robots can coexist. Each of these works, however, fixes
a single safety formulation in advance, usually a distance constraint or a penalty. It remains open which formulation
fits the most robots into a fixed arena with minimal collisions.
Safety formulations differ in how collision avoidance is encoded as a constraint in the OCP. Such a constraint is
classically formulated using a barrier function ℎ(𝑥) whose safe set  = {𝑥 ∣ ℎ(𝑥) ≥ 0} is kept forward invariant
to certify safety [29], typically imposed as a hard [30] or soft [31] distance constraint. A Control Barrier Function
(CBF) [29] relaxes this strict boundary condition by allowing ℎ(𝑥) to decrease at a rate bounded by a class- function,
and its discrete-time form (Dt-CBF) [32] imposes this at each MPC stage. Zeng et al. [33] enforces it as a hard
constraint, which is often infeasible. Feasibility is restored by softening the decay with slack variables [34, 35, 36],
adding terminal barrier terms [37, 38], or anchoring a soft CBF with one hard step [39]. Beyond feasibility, when the
constraint has relative degree greater than one, as for a distance constraint under unicycle dynamics, a higher-order CBF
(HOCBF) [40, 41] chains the barrier conditions. Lu et al. [39] compare a distance-constraint MPC, a hard discrete-time
CBF, a slacked CBF, and their combination on a common footing, but for a single robot. In the multi-robot setting, safety
is enforced by reactive schemes [42, 43], by post-hoc CBF-QP (quadratic program) filters [44], or by a CBF inside a
decentralized MPC with neighbors as predicted trajectories [45, 46, 47, 48]. A formulation’s collision performance,
however, likely changes with the swarm density. A comparison across densities is required to find the best one.
In this work, we investigate the maximum number of miniature robots a distributed predictive controller can flock in
a fixed arena, and design a controller to push that limit. Each robot solves its own NMPC combining Reynolds cohesion,
alignment, and separation (gated by neighbor presence) with inter-robot/obstacle safety and the task objective. It selects
the 𝐾 nearest neighbors within the communication radius and exchanges predicted trajectories synchronously as OCP
parameters, giving a fully distributed, leader-free architecture with no central planner or global map. We evaluate it
on a physics-informed digital twin [49] of the GRASPion [50], an open-source vibration-driven bristlebot studied as
an active-matter model system. We characterize how densely the swarm packs a 0.64 m2 arena while sustaining two
behaviors. These are phototaxis (ascending toward a moving Gaussian light gradient) and a phototactic orbital mode
(orbiting the source along the intensity level-set). We compare six inter-robot safety formulations on a common footing
(Section 3.3). A slacked barrier paired with a discrete-time CBF at a forward handle [51] sustains the highest density
with the fewest collisions. The main contributions of this paper are as follows:
1. A distributed predictive control system for dense swarms of miniature vibration-driven robots. Each robot solves
its own NMPC fusing flocking, safety, and the task objective, coupled to neighbors only through exchanged
predicted trajectories.
2. Demonstration that a single controller generalizes across qualitatively different collective behaviors. The same
architecture produces gradient-ascending phototaxis, stable phototactic orbital formation, and generic flocking
with no modification to the safety or coordination layers, showing that task objectives and coordination structure
are separable and that new tasks can be added without re-deriving safety guarantees.
3. A careful comparison of six inter-robot safety formulations within one identical controller, isolating the safety
constraint as the only varying factor. The Look-ahead Slack Dt-CBF gives the best overall trade-off for the
phototaxis deployment target.
