Abstract
The neighborhood topology of particle swarm optimization (PSO) determines
information propagation and regulates the exploration–exploitation balance.
However, existing evaluation methods based on fitness and spatial diversity
fail to characterize the dynamic evolution of the population’s internal interaction structure. To address this gap, this paper focuses on small-scale
PSO (20–50 particles) with regular topologies and models the iterative process as a dynamic weighted influence graph. We construct a multi-scale
information-flow evaluation framework covering micro-, meso-, and macrolevel metrics, and propose a freeze-point detection method based on effective nodes, a macro-level distributional metric. Experiments on CEC2017
benchmark functions show that the information flow structure freezes early
in the search (3%–22% for representative configurations; 15%–49% for global
medians) and remains unchanged thereafter. The freeze time follows the
order Gbest < Von Neumann < Ring, consistent across standard, LDIW,
and TVAC variants. Parameter-adaptive variants delay freezing without altering the topological order, and the freeze point is significantly negatively

1. Introduction
Particle swarm optimization (PSO), proposed by Kennedy and Eberhart
in 1995 [1], is one of the most representative swarm intelligence algorithms.
Owing to its simple structure, few parameters, and ease of implementation,
PSO has been successfully applied to numerous complex optimization problems, such as molecular simulation, inverse design of nanostructures, power
grid dispatch, and graph clustering [2, 3]. However, despite its widespread
success in engineering practice, PSO has long been confronted with the inherent bottleneck of premature convergence when solving high-dimensional,
multimodal, and dynamic optimization problems. Although numerous improved variants have been proposed, the intrinsic operational mechanisms of
PSO, particularly the influence of the neighborhood topology on the algorithm, remain insufficiently understood [4].
The neighborhood topology determines the paths, speed, and range of
information propagation among particles, and is a core factor affecting the
balance between exploration and exploitation. The canonical PSO adopts
a fully connected (Gbest) topology, which enables fast information diffusion
but rapidly erodes population diversity. The ring (Ring) topology maintains
diversity but often suffers from slow convergence. Kennedy et al. compared several static topologies and found that the Von Neumann topology
generally achieved the best consistency, whereas the fully connected topology performed worst [5]. Going a step further, Blackwell et al. conducted

systematic experiments and confirmed this trade-off, showing that Gbest is
generally the better algorithm for unimodal and separable problems, whereas
a Ring neighborhood of connectivity two (Lbest) is the preferred choice for
multimodal, non-separable, and composition functions [6]. However, these
topologies are all predefined static structures that remain unchanged during
the search process, and their structural influence on the search process has
not yet been fully characterized. More importantly, theoretical analyses of
the impact of topology on the convergence of PSO remain scarce.
However, traditional theoretical analyses of PSO have paid insufficient
attention to the structural role of topology. Existing theoretical work mainly
characterizes the mathematical properties of particle trajectories through
stochastic convergence analysis and Lyapunov stability analysis [7], but these
analyses are typically built upon simplified assumptions, such as assuming a
fully connected (Gbest) population or ignoring the influence of neighborhood
topology on information propagation paths. As pointed out in a recent study
[8], many theorems assume that the expectation and variance of each particle
exist and converge, which is rarely satisfied in multimodal and dynamic scenarios. More importantly, these analyses focus on the motion of individual
particles rather than the population-level information interaction structure.
Questions such as how topology affects information propagation, when it
stops changing, and how it causally relates to search performance are difficult to answer within the traditional theoretical framework. This theoretical
gap motivates the need for an alternative perspective that characterizes the
structural dynamics of PSO populations during actual algorithm runs.
In recent years, network science has emerged as a promising lens for analyzing PSO dynamics. Oliveira et al. made pioneering contributions by
proposing the concept of a swarm influence graph [9]. They further introduced the weighted influence graph by aggregating interaction records over
a sliding window [10], and used network metrics such as the R-value and
the number of zero eigenvalues of the Laplacian matrix to diagnose stagnation [11]. More recently, Deng et al. [8] systematically examined the
intrinsic topology of PSO and found that PSO populations inherently exhibit a small-world architecture and a heavy-tailed degree distribution. The
heavy-tailed property confirms the existence of hub nodes that dominate
information propagation. Despite these advances, existing studies have either provided modeling tools and a posteriori diagnostics or described static
topological properties, whereas the dynamic evolution of information flow
structures and the point at which this evolution ceases to change have not

Motivated by this gap, this paper investigates the freezing mechanism
and topology dependence of information flow structure in small-scale PSO
with regular neighborhood topologies. Particularly, we focus on the typical
population size of PSO (20–50 particles), three regular topologies (Gbest,
Ring, and Von Neumann), and three conventional PSO variants (standard,
LDIW [12], and TVAC [13]). The scope is deliberately restricted to this
regime for three reasons. First, 20–50 particles is the most common population size in practical PSO implementations, making this regime directly
relevant to real-world applications. Second, regular topologies provide a controlled experimental setting: node degrees, community structure, and path
lengths are all predetermined, and the only varying factor is the distribution
of edge weights. This allows us to isolate information flow concentration as
a single variable and clearly observe its evolution and freezing. Third, conventional variants allow us to examine the freezing phenomenon without the
confounding influence of sophisticated structural adaptive mechanisms.
To be specific, this paper establishes a dynamic network-based framework
to analyze the evolution of information flow in small-scale PSO. More precisely, the PSO population is modeled as a dynamic weighted influence graph,
in which particles are represented as nodes and interaction weights characterize the influence strength among particles. Based on this representation,
we construct a multi-scale information-flow evaluation framework comprising micro-, meso-, and macro-level metrics. The micro-level and meso-level
structures are largely determined by the predefined regular topology, whereas
the macro-level metrics describe the overall distribution of influence and thus
can reflect structural changes during the search. Among these metrics, we
construct the effective number of nodes, Strength Gini, and Strength Entropy to characterize the macro-level distribution of information flow. On
this basis, we propose a freeze-point detection method that defines the freeze
point as the first sample at which the effective number of nodes series remains
statistically constant over a sliding window, thereby providing a quantitative
and operational criterion for structural stagnation.
Subsequently, systematic experiments are conducted on the CEC2017
benchmark functions [14] with small-scale swarms of 20 to 50 particles and
three regular topologies, namely Gbest, Von Neumann, and Ring. Three
PSO variants are further considered, including standard PSO, LDIW, and
TVAC. These experiments investigate when the information flow structure
freezes, how the freeze time depends on topology and algorithmic variant,
